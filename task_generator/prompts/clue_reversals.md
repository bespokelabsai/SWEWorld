## What to write

**One remark per earlier decision listed above.** In it, somebody says plainly
that the old decision is gone and what took its place.

A reader has to be able to tell, from this remark alone, that the earlier one no
longer holds. That is the entire job. Today a reader meets a confident January
remark and a confident May remark that disagree, with nothing to say which one
won — and the January one is the more specific and the more quotable of the two,
so it is the one they follow.

Three things the remark must do:

1. **Name the thing being dropped, concretely.** "the metadata_{i}.json plan",
   "sweeping before we plan" — the same nouns the earlier remark used, so the two
   line up for anyone who reads both. A reversal of something unnamed reverses
   nothing.
2. **Say it is dropped, in the past tense, as a fact.** "we moved that", "that's
   gone", "we stopped doing that". Not "we should probably", not "I think we
   might" — this is a report of a change that already happened.
3. **Say what replaced it,** precisely enough to act on, using the exact
   identifiers from the requirement above.

**Give the reason it was dropped when there is one.** A reversal with a cause —
"it wiped a working run when planning threw" — is what makes a reader trust the
new decision over the old one instead of splitting the difference between them.

**Do not narrate the reversal as bookkeeping.** "Reverting the decision from
January" is a changelog entry, not something a person says in chat. They say what
broke and what they do now.

Never use the words "herring", "clue", "planted", or "requirement". Nobody in this
company knows those remarks were written for a task.

One or two sentences, under forty words, in that person's voice.
